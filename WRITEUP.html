1. Summary

Given a parent drug as a SMILES string, this project returns up to five candidate metabolite structures. I compared generating structures with Qwen3-8B and using it to rank candidates produced by chemical reaction rules.

The experiments address three questions:

  1. Can the model write metabolites from scratch, or does it do better picking from a list of rule-generated candidates?
  2. Does adding mammalian GOSTAR records improve the ranker?
  3. Does a separate adapter predicting the site of metabolism (SOM) make it better?

Methods: for the ranking model in hypothesis #1, we use 42 SMIRKS rules to enumerate every metabolite candidate those rules can produce for a given parent (median of 32 candidates per parent). Qwen3-8B, fine-tuned with LoRA, answers yes/no on each and returns the five highest on logP(yes) − logP(no). The base training corpus contained 4,276 pairs from DrugBank, GLORYx and MetXBioDB. A second ranking run added mammalian GOSTAR records. A separate adapter was trained on available site annotations, including mammalian GOSTAR. It outputs the parent molecule with the predicted reacting atoms marked.

The main comparison uses the provided test set of 309 pairs. Recall@5 is the share of recorded metabolites appearing in the top five under standardised matching, which removes stereochemistry and merges charge and tautomer representations. The test set informed rule development, so these results are exploratory. Full metrics accompany the results in Section 3.

Results. Answers to my three hypotheses:

  1. The trained ranker recovered 46.0% of recorded metabolites, compared with 30.7% for the generator reused as a ranker and 21.4% for direct generation. The untuned yes/no control scored 16.2%. These compare complete training and scoring recipes; the objective alone was not isolated.
  2. Adding mammalian GOSTAR records increased ranking examples from 24,025 to 60,520 and moved recall@5 from 46.0% to 46.6%, two additional reference rows. Each mixture was trained once. Both selected checkpoints were at epoch 1.5. The small development probe and changing data composition and update count leave data saturation unresolved.
  3. Using site labels derived from the known answers raises recall from 46.6% to 61.2%. The SOM adapter matches at least one labelled site for 62.6% of evaluated drugs. Filtering on its predicted sites reduces recall to 32.4%. A development-selected score adjustment that retains all candidates reaches 47.9%, compared with its 46.9% baseline. This small gain remains uncertain.

Other findings: This small comparison of untuned models showed no consistent relationship between model size and recall. Seven models from 4B to 397B parameters were tested on 120 molecules, with recall@5 ranging from 15.8% to 24.2%. Model families and training variants also differed. Qwen3-8B was chosen for its cost and the baselines already available.

Pooling the model's own generated structures into the candidate list from SMIRKS reactions dropped recall from 46.9% to 29.8%. Every training negative came from a reaction rule, so unfamiliar generated candidates are one possible explanation. That mechanism was not tested directly.

We tested an alternative SMILES format by adding spaces between chemical units. A short development pilot found no clear benefit from spaced SMILES: it recovered 1 of 108 reference metabolites, compared with 3 for standard SMILES, and used 48% more training tokens. Other chemistry-based tokenization methods could be tested next.

2. Methods

Data and evaluation. We combined DrugBank, GLORYx and MetXBioDB, converted structures to canonical SMILES and removed duplicate pairs. We excluded carbon-free parents, parents with over 100 heavy atoms, and pairs whose metabolite matched the unchanged parent. We also removed stereochemistry from metabolite targets and any resulting duplicates.

We split the cleaned data 90:10 by standardized parent identity, giving 4,276 training pairs and 437 development pairs. The provided 309 test pairs were excluded from weight fitting and checkpoint selection. Their missed transformations informed reaction-rule development. A fresh cohort must be reserved before further tuning to estimate generalisation independently.

For generator training, each parent had up to four SMILES representations, including its canonical form and randomized atom orderings. These described the same molecule and shared one fixed metabolite target. This expanded training to 17,075 examples, with the aim of reducing sensitivity to how the input SMILES was written. GOSTAR additions were restricted to mammalian records, with invalid structures, duplicate pairs and development/test parent overlaps removed.

Our primary metric was recall@5, the fraction of recorded metabolites found among the five highest-ranked predictions. Matching normalises functional groups, neutralises charges, selects a canonical tautomer and removes stereochemistry. On identical cached predictions, recall@5 is 33.7% with exact canonical matching, 44.7% after removing stereo, and 46.9% with full standardisation. The endpoint measures recovery under that identity policy; it cannot establish recall over unrecorded metabolites.

2.1 Prediction pipeline

We compared generating metabolites directly with ranking candidates produced by 42 editable chemical transformation rules encoded as SMIRKS. With five predictions allowed per drug, the highest achievable recall@5 from these candidates was 71.5% on the test set.

The trained ranker scored each drug–candidate pair independently using log P(yes) − log P(no) and returned the five highest-scoring candidates.

Separate LoRA adapters were trained for generation and ranking using the literature training corpus. These tasks produced 17,075 generation examples and 24,025 ranking examples. Adding GOSTAR expanded the ranking dataset to 60,520 examples, approximately 2.5 times its original size, to test whether additional training data improved performance.

Note: Candidates labelled “no” during training may include real metabolites that are not recorded for that drug in the datasets.

2.2 Model configurations and comparison goals

We compared seven configurations to test whether fine-tuning improves metabolite prediction, how generation compares with candidate ranking, and whether additional training data helps. All configurations used Qwen3-8B, the same test drugs and the same evaluation procedure.

2.3 Choosing the saved model

We selected generation and ranking checkpoints with the highest recall@5 on fixed development subsets. Recall@5 measures the fraction of recorded metabolites recovered within five predictions. Cross-entropy loss measures how well the model predicts the tokens in the recorded answer strings.

The figure shows how the generator’s performance changed during training. We measured development loss on 160 examples and recall@5 on a separate subset of 60 parent structures containing 98 recorded drug–metabolite pairs. Development loss was lowest after one epoch, when the model recovered 19.4% of the recorded metabolites within its five predictions. Recovery reached 28.6% after four epochs, an improvement of 9.2 percentage points. We selected this four-epoch checkpoint because it recovered the most recorded metabolites on the development subset.

During this period, training loss continued to fall and development loss increased. This suggests that the model was becoming more closely fitted to its training examples, with poorer prediction of the development answer strings. The improvement in metabolite recovery therefore needs to be considered alongside this evidence of possible overfitting.

3.1 Training for ranking gave the strongest results

Fine-tuned generation recovered 21.4% of recorded metabolites. Untuned generation recovered 4.9% with six examples in the prompt and 1.3% without examples.

Using the fine-tuned generator to rank rule-generated candidates increased recall to 30.7%, using the same model weights. Untuned likelihood ranking reached 20.4%.

Training specifically for yes/no ranking reached 46.0%, compared with 16.2% for the untuned yes/no control. This approach combined changes to training examples, scoring and model selection.

3.2 Additional data and candidates

Adding GOSTAR increased the ranking dataset approximately 2.5-fold and moved recall from 46.0% to 46.6%, recovering two additional metabolites. Both runs reached their highest measured development recall around 1.5 training epochs. Each configuration was trained once, so the reliability of this small gain remains uncertain. The selected checkpoints each recovered 33 of 68 development pairs, the maximum of nine checkpoint evaluations per run. Total optimizer updates were 2,020 and 5,060, including smoke runs.

3.3 Metabolite coverage and recall by SMIRKS reaction rule

The 42 SMIRKS rules did not cover 86 of 309 recorded metabolites, leaving those answers unavailable to the ranker. For the base ranker, the law of total probability gives:

46.0% overall recall@5 = 72.2% rule coverage × 63.7% recall among reachable metabolites

Expanding the rules to cover common missing transformations could make more metabolites available for prediction. Any increase in overall recall would also depend on how accurately the ranker orders the additional candidates. Among the 24 missed aliphatic hydroxylations, 13 had another product of the same rule in the top five, with hydroxylation at a different position. This error slice motivates testing whether site-of-metabolism predictions improve candidate ordering.

3.4 Using Site of Metabolism (SOM) Information

A site of metabolism (SOM) identifies where a chemical change occurs in the parent molecule. For each drug, we used sites recorded for its known metabolites to keep only candidates generated by rules acting at those sites. The same ranker then ordered the remaining candidates, increasing recall@5 from 46.6% to 61.2%. Knowing the reaction sites therefore substantially improved prediction in this experiment.

We then used a separate model to predict the sites. Filtering with these predictions kept only 105 of 223 reachable reference metabolites and reduced recall@5 to 32.4%. This suggests that the site predictions were too incomplete or inaccurate to use as a hard filter.

We then kept all candidates and used the predicted sites as a soft prior. Candidates supported by a predicted site received a score bonus controlled by the prior weight, λ = 0.75:

The indicator is 1 when a candidate's reacting atoms overlap a predicted site, and 0 otherwise.

The prior increased recall@5 from 46.9% to 47.9%, a gain of approximately one percentage point. Its 95% confidence interval spans −0.7 to +2.7 percentage points and includes zero, so the improvement remains uncertain. The interval resamples 154 standardised parent clusters, retaining all reference rows for each sampled drug. It is conditional on the saved predictions and excludes variation from retraining or rule selection.

4. Discussion and next steps

4.1 Chemistry: candidate coverage and reaction sites

The current rules cannot reach 27.8% of reference pairs. We would repair incorrect rule products and expand missing transformations, starting with hydroxylation. Each rule should pass atom-mapping and product checks. We would also review the ranker's top-five errors to build harder training examples, then test training it to compare candidates from the same drug. Unrecorded products would remain uncertain labels unless further evidence resolves their status.

Known reaction sites improved recall. Filtering with predicted sites removed many reachable answers. We would improve site prediction and train the ranker with site-marked parent structures, keeping all candidates. Training-site predictions should come from models that did not train on those drugs, so the ranker learns from realistic prediction errors.

4.2 AI engineering: molecular representation and training

Our small pilot added spaces between SMILES units using Qwen's existing vocabulary. It showed no clear recall gain and used more tokens. A short output-budget check would compare 96- and 192-token limits on the same generator with matched sampling seeds. We would then repeat the representation test with longer training and several training seeds, checking that training and inference use the same encoding.

After the data and evaluation checks, experiments could test learned chemical fragments as tokens, as in SMILES Pair Encoding, with compatible model embeddings and output layers. We would also test a short training stage on molecular structure tasks, such as ring counting, before fine-tuning the ranker, following CLEANMOL. Their benefit for metabolite ranking remains untested. Compare each change against the same baseline, tracking recall, valid outputs, runtime and token cost.

Three-month sequence: Month 1 would cover data and rule repairs, a reserved evaluation set, and reviewed ranking errors. Month 2 would test learned SOM inputs and selected LLM changes, including a small RL pilot. Month 3 would repeat promising runs and evaluate the fixed pipeline on the reserved set.

Time spent: about 30 to 40 hours over four to five days. The two ranking fine-tunes account for roughly 13 of those on their own and ran unattended.
