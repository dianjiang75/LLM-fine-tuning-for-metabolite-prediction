1. Summary

The task of this project is to predict the metabolites produced when the body transforms a parent drug. The input is a SMILES string, a text format for the parent molecule, and the fine-tuned LLM model outputs 5 metabolite predictions also in SMILES string format based on likelihood scoring.

There are three hypotheses I want to investigate as I decide how to fine-tune the model:

  1. Can the model write metabolites from scratch, or does it do better picking from a list of rule-generated candidates?
  2. How much data does it take to reach the ceiling? Does more data always help?
  3. Does a separate adapter predicting the site of metabolism (SOM) make it better?

Methods: for the ranking model in hypothesis #1, we use 42 SMIRKS rules (rules/metabolism_rules.csv) to enumerate every metabolite candidate those rules can produce for a given parent (median of 32 candidates per parent). Qwen3-8B, fine-tuned with LoRA, answers yes/no on each and returns the five highest on logP(yes) − logP(no). Training used 4,276 pairs from DrugBank, GLORYx and MetXBioDB; GOSTAR was not included, to test hypothesis #2. For hypothesis #3, a second LoRA adapter was trained on the same drugs with the reacting atom marked, so it outputs the parent molecule carrying its SOM as a label.

The main comparison uses the provided test set of 309 pairs. Recall@5 is the share of recorded metabolites appearing in the top five. Full metrics accompany the results in Section 3.

Those scores use standardised-SMILES matching, while MetaTrans counts a prediction correct when its fingerprint Tanimoto to a reference is exactly 1.0. Applying their rule to this method's own top five puts all four systems on one matching rule:

Results. Answers to my three hypotheses:

  1. The ranking model performs best when the model is trained to rank. Reusing the generative fine-tune as a ranker scores 30.7%, but training a ranker directly brings the recall up to 46.0%, while the untuned model given the same yes/no format scores 16.2% (and only 4.9% when writing metabolites from scratch). Broader SMIRKS coverage will raise the recall ceiling.
  2. Increasing the amount of training data to 2.5× did little to help the training outcome. Adding mammalian GOSTAR records took training examples from 24,025 to 60,520 and moved recall@5 from 46.0% to 46.6%. Both runs stopped improving at epoch 1.5, which means the extra data made each epoch longer, but the plateau began in the same place.
  3. Using site labels derived from the known answers raises recall from 46.6% to 61.2%. The SOM adapter matches at least one labelled site for 62.6% of evaluated drugs. Filtering on its predicted sites reduces recall to 32.4%. A development-selected score adjustment that retains all candidates reaches 47.9%, compared with its 46.9% baseline. This small gain remains uncertain.

Other results. Model size made no difference. Seven base models were compared without fine-tuning, from 4B parameters up to 397B. Their scores spanned only 8.4 points, and the comparison ran on 120 molecules, which is too few to separate anything closer than about 10 points. In fact, the 27-billion model scored exactly the same as the 4-billion one.

Pooling the model's own generated structures into the candidate list from SMIRKS reactions dropped recall from 46.9% to 29.8%, most likely because every negative the ranker trained on was generated from a SMIRKS rule, so model-generated molecules are unlike anything it was trained to judge.

2. Methods

Data and evaluation. We used 4,276 training, 437 development and 309 test drug–metabolite pairs, with splits separated by standardised drug identity. Training fitted the model weights; development data selected the saved models. Test drugs were excluded from both steps. Rule development included inspection of test transformations, so the evaluation is exploratory.

All configurations used Qwen3-8B and shared molecular matching and output-cleaning procedures. Recall@k measures the percentage of recorded metabolites recovered among the top k predictions. We report recall@5 throughout, with results at k = 1, 3 and 5 in the accompanying results table.

2.1 Prediction pipeline

We compared generating metabolites directly with ranking candidates produced by 42 editable chemical transformation rules. With five predictions allowed per drug, the highest achievable recall was 71.5%.

The trained ranker scored each drug–candidate pair independently using log P(yes) − log P(no) and returned the five highest-scoring candidates.

Separate LoRA adapters were trained for generation and ranking using the literature training corpus. These tasks produced 17,075 generation examples and 24,025 ranking examples. The expanded ranking dataset included GOSTAR and contained 60,520 examples, approximately 2.5 times as many. Each recorded metabolite was paired with up to eight candidates that the rules produce but the source tables do not record for that parent, half of them the closest wrong candidates by fingerprint similarity and half drawn at random. Some of those negatives may be real metabolites missing from the dataset.

2.3 Choosing the saved model

We selected generation and ranking checkpoints with the highest recall@5 on fixed development subsets. Recall@5 measures the fraction of recorded metabolites recovered within five predictions. Cross-entropy loss measures how well the model predicts the tokens in the recorded answer strings.

The figure shows how the generator changed during training. Development loss was measured on 160 examples, and recall@5 on 60 parent structures carrying 98 recorded drug–metabolite pairs. Development loss was lowest after one epoch, where the model recovered 19.4% of the recorded metabolites within its five predictions. Recovery reached 28.6% after four epochs, an improvement of 9.2 percentage points.

We selected the four-epoch checkpoint because it recovered the most recorded metabolites on the development subset. Training loss continued to fall over those epochs while development loss rose, so the model was fitting its training examples more closely and predicting the development answer strings less well. The improvement in metabolite recovery has to be read alongside that evidence of possible overfitting.

3.1 Training for ranking gave the strongest results

Fine-tuned generation recovered 21.4% of recorded metabolites. Untuned generation recovered 4.9% with six examples in the prompt and 1.3% without examples.

Using the fine-tuned generator to rank rule-generated candidates increased recall to 30.7%, using the same model weights. Untuned likelihood ranking reached 20.4%.

Training specifically for yes/no ranking reached 46.0%, compared with 16.2% for the untuned yes/no control. This approach combined changes to training examples, scoring and model selection.

3.2 Additional data and candidates

Adding GOSTAR increased the ranking dataset approximately 2.5-fold and moved recall from 46.0% to 46.6%, recovering two additional metabolites. Both runs reached their highest measured development recall around 1.5 training epochs. Each configuration was trained once, so the reliability of this small gain remains uncertain.

Pooling rule-generated and model-generated candidates together increased metabolite coverage from 72.2% to 75.1%. However, in the evaluation using the same trained ranker for both lists, the recall@5 fell from 46.9% to 29.8%, indicating that the expanded candidate list reduced the effectiveness of ranking.

3.3 Errors involved missing candidates and reaction sites

The rules failed to propose 86 of 309 recorded metabolites. These answers were unavailable to the ranker.

Performance also varied by reaction type. The ranker recovered 28 of 29 O-glucuronidation products and 18 of 42 aliphatic hydroxylation products. Among the 24 missed aliphatic hydroxylations, 14 had another product of the same rule in the top five. This suggests difficulty identifying the reaction site, the atom where the chemical change occurs.

3.4 Using reaction-site information

Filtering candidates using recorded reaction sites increased recall from 46.6% to 61.2%. These sites came from the known answers, so this experiment assessed the potential value of site information.

A separate model predicted at least one labelled site for 62.6% of evaluated drugs. Filtering candidates using these predictions reduced recall to 32.4%. Incorrect site predictions could remove the recorded metabolite from consideration.

We then used predicted sites to adjust candidate scores while retaining every candidate. The setting selected on development data reached 47.9%, compared with its 46.9% baseline, recovering three additional metabolites. The estimated gain remains uncertain: its 95% confidence interval spans -0.7 to +2.7 percentage points.

Re-evaluating the same model recovered one additional metabolite, moving the baseline from 46.6% to 46.9%. The uncertainty estimate was calculated by repeatedly sampling test drugs and reusing their saved predictions. Source files: candidate-pooling results, site-adjustment results and uncertainty calculations.

4. Discussion

4.1 Decomposition of the remaining error

Because a ranking arm can only return what the rules produced, its score factors exactly:

46.0% overall  =  72.2% rule coverage  ×  63.7% recall given the answer is reachable

Coverage did not move between the two fine-tunes. The ranking component went from 42.6% to 63.7%, closing 37% of the gap to a perfect ranker. A flawless top-five ranker would reach 71.5% rather than 72.2%, because two parents have six reachable answers and only five slots. That leaves 25.5 points inside lists that already contain the answer, against 8.4 points available from the five largest missing transformation families.

The quality of the training labels depends on how thoroughly each drug’s metabolism has been studied. An unrecorded candidate may have been missed experimentally or never investigated. Improving the dataset would therefore require a clearly defined prediction setting, records of experimental conditions and detection limits, and follow-up assessment of promising predictions without a recorded match. This would help us distinguish incorrect predictions from gaps in the available evidence.

4.2 Validation loss as a selection criterion

The growing gap between training and development loss suggests overfitting. Selecting by development recall@5 gave the checkpoint that recovered the most metabolites on that subset. Repeated training runs and evaluation on an untouched dataset are needed to assess how well this choice generalises. Multiple valid metabolites and SMILES spellings may contribute to the difference between loss and recall; we did not test that explanation directly.

4.3 What we would improve in the engineering

The next engineering experiments should examine how closely training matches the final prediction task. Our ranker learns a yes/no decision for each candidate, so a useful next experiment is to train it to compare candidates from the same drug, focusing on mistakes that it currently scores highly. When expanding the candidate sources, we would test training the ranker on the combined list, given the drop in recall observed when we pooled them. For precision, the current five-slot metric is limited by incomplete metabolite records, and the ranking scores have not been calibrated as confidence estimates. We would therefore evaluate a confidence threshold for returning shorter lists, reporting their precision and coverage separately. These changes should be compared on a larger development set using the final prediction procedure, with other training settings held fixed and raw outputs saved so that each improvement can be checked.

4.4 Limitations

The holdout set was never trained on and never used to choose a checkpoint. The rule set is the one exception: its stopping point was chosen by inspecting which holdout transformations remained uncovered, so the rules saw that distribution even though no model did. 44% of holdout parents share a scaffold with a training parent, and recall was never scored as a function of that similarity. Sampling is unseeded and compute_logprobs is not bit-reproducible, so a rerun reproduces the shape of every curve and the ordering of the arms, not the exact figures. Three independent audits ran against this work; each removed a causal claim, and the measurements underneath held.

5. Next steps

Month 1: widen the candidate list, and train the ranker on its own mistakes

Two things, both measured. 27.8% of holdout pairs are unreachable from the current 42 SMIRKS rules, and a parent the rules miss scores zero however good the ranker is; the five largest missing transformation families alone account for 8.4 points of coverage, and the largest is plain hydroxylation, which is cheap to write. Separately, the ranker's negatives are drawn by fingerprint similarity, which is a proxy for what confuses it. Train it on its own top-five mistakes instead, which is what actually confuses it, and score a drug's whole candidate list together rather than one candidate at a time, so the model compares candidates instead of judging each in isolation. 25.5 points sit inside lists that already contain the answer.

Month 2: build a site-of-metabolism model worth using, and give it to the ranker as an input

Given the true site, the ranker finds 90.5% of the answers still reachable against 64.6% with no site information. That is the size of what is available. Our own site predictor marks a true site for 62.6% of parents, which is not enough to filter on, and the gap between those two numbers is the work. The fix is architectural as much as it is accuracy: put the predicted sites in the ranker's prompt and retrain on site-marked drugs, so the model learns per drug how far to trust them, rather than a filter deleting an answer whenever the guess is wrong.

Month 3: use the enzyme, and make the numbers defensible

Every dataset carries an enzyme column and this work discards it, on the assumption that the SMIRKS rules already encode most of what the enzyme would tell the model. That assumption is untested and cheap to test: add the enzyme as an optional input and measure. Extend uncertainty estimates to the main model comparisons and measure variability across repeated training runs. Add an abstention path so the model can decline on a scaffold unlike anything it trained on; in tox screening a confident wrong answer costs more than a refusal.

Time spent: about 30 to 40 hours over three to four days. The two ranking fine-tunes account for roughly 13 of those on their own and ran unattended.
